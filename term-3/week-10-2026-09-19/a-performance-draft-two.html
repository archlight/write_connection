Two things to do before you write Draft 2

Twenty-seven marks, and four of them are stars — the most praise on any of your three papers so far. Most of the rest are repairs you can make while copying the story out. These two are different, and they have to be done in this order: you cannot put the feelings in the right place until you have decided what actually happened.

Do first1

Close the three holes where the reader stops believing the scene

Your teacher named this one herself, in the left margin: (Logic gap. Did they continue performing or not?) It happens three times, and each one is a place where a reader trying to picture the stage finds the picture will not hold together. None of them is a spelling or a tense — every sentence is fine on its own. The trouble is in what sits between them.

The three holes, in the order they appear
  • Line 9 — the curtains are about to close. But the performance is starting and the audience wants to see the dancers. Closed curtains are what stops them seeing. She wrote open above it.
  • Line 25 — I clambered to my teeth. You got to your feet. The words came across from the sentence two lines earlier about your teeth chattering. She struck them without writing the replacement, which means she wants you to find it.
  • Line 24 again — what happened to the performance? You fall, and the very next thing on the page is a friend patting you. Is the music still playing? Is everyone still dancing? Did you carry on? She wrote the answer in for you: Immediately, the curtains closed and our performance ended abruptly.
Why the third one matters most

The whole composition is about falling during a performance. What happens to the performance is the story. Leaving it out is like telling someone you dropped a glass and never saying whether it broke. Notice she did not ask you a question and leave it — she wrote the sentence in, which is what a teacher does when a gap is too big to hint at.

and I felt very embbarrassed for the audience. I clambered to my teeth. My friend gently patted me

I felt very embarrassed. I clambered to my feet. Immediately, the curtains closed and our performance ended abruptly. My friend gently patted me

How to find these yourself

Read Draft 2 as though you had not been there. Every time you catch yourself supplying something from memory to make a sentence make sense, that thing needs to be on the page.

Do next2

Move the tears to where the tears belong

The longest remark on the paper is this one: Why would she cry here? Use this description later on instead. Read the moment as a reader gets it. The audience is clapping. Nothing has gone wrong. And you burst into tears like a waterfall. Then later, when you actually fall over in front of everybody, there are no tears at all. The feelings and the events are in opposite places.

She is not telling you to delete it — she is telling you to move it

This is the important part. The waterfall is the best image in the composition, and she says so twice: once by striking it here with use this description later on instead, and once by writing it back in eleven lines further down, beside your friend patting you — as tears fell down my face like a waterfall. Same image, right place.

As the audience clapped and tears came out like a waterfall and (continue sitting down and relax)

As the audience clapped, I continued performing confidently.

My friend gently patted me in encouragedment

My friend gently patted me in encouragement as tears fell down my face like a waterfall.

The shape your feelings should follow
BeforeNerves. You already have these, and they are good — the cold bus, the butterflies, the chattering teeth. Nothing to change.
DuringConfidence, then the fall. Her replacement line gives you the confidence: I continued performing confidently. Let the reader enjoy it for a sentence or two before it goes wrong.
AfterEmbarrassment, then tears. This is where the waterfall goes, and it is where the space is — right now the worst moment of your day gets four words. Give it room.
LastGratitude. Your ending already does this well, and it was starred. Do not touch it.
One more thing worth adding
  • Say why you fell. You write that you lost your balance, but not what caused it — a foot catching the hem, a slippery patch, turning too fast. One clause is enough, and it stops the fall feeling like something the story needed rather than something that happened.
