Two things to do before you write Draft 2

Thirty-two marks, and the single biggest group is not spelling or tense — it is too vague, five times. That is the thread running through this whole draft, and both recommendations pull on it. Do them in this order, because you cannot write the reaction properly until you have decided what the reaction is to.

Do first1

Name the allergy — your title promises it and your story never says it

Her longest margin remark is this one: (What exactly was he allergic to? ‘sauce’ → too vague). The composition is called An allergic reaction, and the one fact it never gives the reader is what the reaction is to. A smoke sauce allergy is not something anybody has — you cannot be allergic to a flavour. She did not hint at this one; she wrote the answer in for you.

What she wrote in, and what it fixes

But I didn’t notice that I have a smoke sauce allergy.

However, I did not notice that the lamb chops had nuts in its sauce. I was allergic to nuts.

Notice she splits it in two: first what was in the food, then what that meant for you. One fact per sentence. And once the allergen has a name, three other things in the story suddenly work — the smell of the sauce becomes a clue, the antihistamine pills in your bag make sense, and the lesson at the end has something real to attach to.

The other four places the reader is left guessing
  • Line 3 — my WA1. School shorthand. Everybody in your class knows it; a marker reading a story does not. She wrote test.
  • Line 5 — let the waiter know our food. Know your food how? She added orders.
  • Line 23 — my pills. She added antihistamine, and that one word quietly tells the reader you already knew about the allergy and came prepared.
  • Line 27 — to let Timmy help me. Her question: (help you do what?) You have already swallowed the medicine, so what is left for him to do? This one she did not answer — it is yours.
The habit underneath all five

Every one of these is a word that was clear in your head and empty on the page. The test is to read each sentence as somebody who was not there, and stop at every noun that could mean five different things.

Do next2

Spend your lines where the story actually is

Count them. Ordering the food and saying thank you take up nine lines. The allergic reaction — the thing the title names, the only thing that goes wrong, the whole reason there is a story — gets from line 17 to line 27. Her margin note (Avoid using too much speech) is about three of those nine lines, and it is really a note about where your words are going.

Thirty words of politeness, cut to eight

chops, I praised my brother while saying “thanks, brother I appreciate to bring me here.” “No problem, as long as you dont eat moderate,” Timmy replied.

chops and thanked my brother for the treat.

Nothing is lost. The reader still knows you were grateful and your brother was pleased. What goes is thirty words of being polite at the exact moment the story should be speeding up.

Dialogue is expensive — spend it where it earns
  • Keep Timmy’s shout. “Ahhhh! You look like you have a million ants on your face!” earns every word — it tells the reader what you looked like from the outside, which you could not have described yourself, and it does it in a panicking voice.
  • Cut the thank-yous. Characters being polite to each other reveals nothing and costs lines.
  • The test: does this speech change something, reveal a feeling, or show the reader something they could not otherwise see? If not, say it in narration in half the words.
Where the lines you save should go
The clueThe smell, before you eat. You already have the aroma of the smokey sauce on the lamb chops filled the air. Once the allergen is nuts, that smell can carry a hint — something faintly sweet underneath it that you did not stop to think about.
The reactionWhat it actually felt like. Right now there is one tingle and then your brother shouts. Give it two or three sentences from the inside: lips, throat, the heat rising in your face, the moment you realise what is happening.
The rescueWhat Timmy did. Her question (help you do what?) is waiting for one specific action — calling for water, telling the waiter, getting you outside into the air. One thing the reader can watch.
