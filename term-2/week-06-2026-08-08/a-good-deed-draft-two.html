Two things to do before you write Draft 2

Twenty-seven marks is a lot to hold in your head at once. Most of them — the spellings, the tenses, the full stops — are repairs you can make as you copy the story out. These two are different: they change what the story is. Do them in this order, because the second one only pays off once the first is in place.

Do first1

Look at the old lady with kindness, not judgement

Your teacher struck out a whole clause here and wrote a note in the margin about it: Avoid negative descriptions like this. The words she removed were and so weird, because she is a elderly lady. This is a composition called A good deed, and at the moment the narrator looks at the person he is about to help and finds her weird for being old. Everything after that is affected — a kind act done by someone who thinks the other person is strange stops being kindness.

What she replaced it with, and why that is the whole lesson

She was holding a walking stick and she looks so lonely and his hands was wrincked and so weird, because she is a elderly lady.

She was holding a walking stick and she looked so lonely. Her hands were wrinkled, gripping her walking stick tightly.

Not one opinion in the replacement. Just a pair of hands and what they were doing. Gripping tightly lets the reader work out for themselves that she is frightened, or unsteady, or both — and a reader who works something out feels it, where a reader who is told simply nods.

Where else this needs doing
  • Line 12 — so lonely. Not unkind, but still a label. What did she do that made you think it? She was approaching passers-by, and they were walking on. That is the loneliness, and it is already in your story one line earlier.
  • Line 22 — she probably had dementia. Fine as a realisation, but let the reader reach it just before you say it. Give them the detail that made you think it — asking the same question twice, or not knowing which way to turn.
  • Give her something to say. She has no voice anywhere in the draft. One line of speech from her would do more than any description — and it is what makes a reader care whether she gets home.

Do next2

Write the ending you skipped — your teacher has already planned it

The longest remark on the paper is a numbered list of three, and beside it she wrote Unclear sequence of events. Read your last few lines as a stranger would: a boy takes an old lady to a police station, and then, with no warning, there is a daughter whose anxious expression vanishes. That daughter has never arrived. She has never been introduced. The reunion, the relief and the thanks — the entire point the story has been walking towards — happen in the gap between two sentences.

Her three numbered prompts are three paragraphs
Ending 1Who she saw waiting for her. The daughter arrives at the station. Let the reader see the door open and see her come in — out of breath, still in her work clothes, phone in her hand.
Ending 2What the daughter felt. This is where the anxious expression vanished finally belongs — after we have watched her be anxious. Her shoulders dropping, her voice breaking, kneeling down to take her mother’s hands.
Ending 3The daughter thanking you. She turns to you and says something. One or two lines of speech. This is the moment your last sentence — my good deed had made a difference — is claiming, so the reader needs to have seen it happen.
One more thing the reader is missing
  • Why you went through her bag. Your teacher wrote to find her identity card into the line for you, and asked (Why did he do this?) in the margin. Keep her four words. Without them, a boy searching a confused old lady’s handbag reads as something the story does not intend.
  • Split the tangled sentence. The old lady felt a anxious expressions on her dauters face vanished is two sentences folded into one, which is why it was marked awkward. One event per sentence, in the order the events happened.
