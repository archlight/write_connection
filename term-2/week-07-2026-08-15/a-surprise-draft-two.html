Two things to do before you write Draft 2

Twenty-something marks is a lot to hold in your head at once. Almost all of them are surface repairs you can make as you copy the story out. These two are different: they change what the story is, so do them first — and do them in this order, because the second one only works once the first is in place.

Do first1

Rebuild the middle from inside your own head, not the teacher’s

Your teacher boxed “As my…” and wrote the reason twice: the camera is pointing at the wrong person. Lines 8–15 follow what the teacher announces, what the teacher secretly looks at, and what the teacher privately worries about — none of which a boy sitting at his desk could possibly see. Meanwhile the boy himself, who is the one taking the test, does nothing for eight straight lines.

Your teacher already wrote the plan — use her three prompts as three paragraphs
Middle 1What you did to get through the test. Gripping the pencil, the question you got stuck on, checking the clock, rubbing out an answer twice, the sound of everyone else writing.
Middle 2How you felt when you finished. The shoulders dropping, the long breath out, that light feeling — and then the boredom of waiting while others finish.
Middle 3How you spotted the box. This is where the surprise is planted. You see a box on the teacher’s table. You do not see a puppy. Wonder about it. Let the reader wonder with you.
Cut these two things completely
  • The secret puppy on line 12. If the reader knows there is a puppy in the box, there is no surprise left for the ending — and your narrator has no way of knowing it. Replace it with the box.
  • Lines 12–15, the whole bracket your teacher marked Unclear. Nobody can tell who he and she are. Those sentences belong to a different story. Delete them and use the space for Middle 1.
What the join might sound like

Before the test, my teacher told us she had a memorable surprise for us after the test. She announced the rules, and then the room went quiet except for the scratching of pencils. I read question one twice before my hand would move…

When I finally put down my pencil, my shoulders dropped and I let out a long breath. That was when I noticed it — a large cardboard box sitting on my teacher’s table, one corner of the lid lifting and settling, lifting and settling, as if something inside were breathing.

Do next2

Give the surprise itself more room than the rules of the test

Count the lines. The teacher announcing the rules gets four. The moment the box opens — the whole reason the story is called A Surprise — gets a single word: “Surprise!” Then “Yahh!”, then happy, and it is over. Your teacher asked three separate questions in that spot: what was the surprise? · describe the puppy · she exclaimed while revealing… That is her telling you the climax is under-written.

Stretch the reveal across five beats instead of one line
  • Slow the lid. One sentence between the hands touching the box and the lid coming off. This is where you make the reader lean in.
  • Sound before sight. A whimper, a scratching, a small squeak from inside — heard a second before anything is seen.
  • Describe the puppy properly. Your teacher asked for this by name. Not a puppy — the colour of its fur, how big it was in her hands, the floppy ear, the wet nose, the tail that would not stop.
  • Show, do not tell, the reaction. She has already written you the model: Our faces lit up with bright smiles. Copy that pattern — a body part, doing something. Nobody in Draft 2 should just be happy.
  • Land the wish on the puppy. Your last line wished for the day twice. Wish for something the story earned instead — that is what the remark “hope or wish could be more meaningful to the plot” is asking for.
