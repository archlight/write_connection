# Marking codes

What each code on the paper means, and how to explain it to the child. Append new
codes as they appear — note the week you first saw one so its reading can be
checked against later papers.

Codes are read from the marking, not from a published key. Where the teacher's
classroom wording differs, hers is authoritative — say so on the page rather than
asserting an expansion.

| Code | Reading | First seen |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `SP` | Spelling | T2 W7 |
| `T` | Tense | T2 W7 |
| `PV` / `pv` | Powerful verb | T2 W7 |
| `G` | Grammar — pronouns, apostrophes, word order | T2 W7 |
| `CI` | Character's inside view — thoughts, feelings, what they notice | T2 W7 |
| `4Ws` | Where, when, what (and who) — the setting is missing | T2 W7 |
| `SWAB` | Start With A Bang — a strong opening. A *positive* mark | T2 W7 |
| Show, not Tell | Prove the feeling with a detail instead of naming it | T2 W7 |
| `✳` | Star — something done well | T2 W7 |

Unlabelled interventions are common too: an arrow to a margin question, a boxed
phrase, a bracket around several lines, a caret marking an insertion point. Give
these a short plain-word code of your own (`Cut`, `Specify`, `Reveal`, `Unclear`,
`Ending`) so the page reads consistently, and make clear in *the mark* field that
she used an arrow rather than a code.

## How to teach each one

### SP — spelling

A correction alone is worth little; the child will misspell it again next week.
Give a **structural hook** — how the word is built, or a word already known that
shares its shape.

- `surprice` → **surprise**. A surprise is a kind of **prize** — and *prize* never
  takes a c.
- `finnally` → **finally**. **final** + **ly**. One n; the double letter is the l.
- `mumured` → **murmured**. The same three letters twice: **mur** · **mur** · ed.
- `stealthly` → **stealthily**. **stealth** + **i** + **ly** — the same shape as
  *happily*, *angrily*, *hungrily*: y turns to i before the ly.
- `uncontrolably` → **uncontrollably**. **control** + **able** doubles the l, then
  **un** in front and **y** on the end.
- `there` / `their` / `they're`. **There** has *here* inside it — a place.
  **Their** has *heir* inside it — an heir owns things. **They're** is always
  *they are*.

### T — tense

Nearly always a present-tense verb dropped into a past-tense story. Two things
worth telling the child:

- The slips cluster in the most exciting paragraph, in every draft by every
  writer, because that is where they write fastest. So read that paragraph twice.
- Do one pass of the whole draft reading **only the verbs**. Nothing else.

### PV — powerful verb

The rule underneath it: *if you need an adverb to prop up a verb, the verb is too
weak.* Give the test — cover the adverb; if the sentence loses its feeling, swap
the verb rather than keeping the adverb.

Always supply a small bank of alternatives (five or six), because "use a stronger
verb" is only actionable if you can think of one.

### G — grammar

Most often subject pronouns (`Me and my friends`), possessive apostrophes
(`teachers desk`) or plurals. Teach the **test**, not the rule:

- Pronouns: take the other people away. *Me were doing* fails, so it is *I*.
  Works in reverse too — *she gave it to my friends and I* → *to I* fails.
- Apostrophes: say it as *the desk of the teacher*. If that works, the apostrophe
  goes in, straight after the owner.

### CI — character's inside view

The structural one, and usually the most important mark on the paper. A story
told as *I* can only contain what *I* can see, hear, think and feel. Look for
whole passages where someone other than the narrator is doing all the acting, and
for things the narrator has no way of knowing.

When the teacher numbers prompts in the margin (*1. what he did… 2. how he
felt… 3. how he saw…*), that is a paragraph plan, not a list of suggestions.
Present it as one.

### 4Ws — where, when, what

The opening has to put the reader somewhere. Push past the building: *in school*
is where; *in the stuffy school hall, under a fan that did not work* is where plus
atmosphere.

### Show, not Tell

Where the teacher writes a model sentence, study its **shape** with the child and
name the pattern. *Our faces lit up with bright smiles* = a body part + a verb +
a detail. That formula transfers to every emotion — fear becomes *my hands turned
cold and would not stay still*.

### Positive marks

Say precisely what was good and why it is unusual. *"Most drafts open with 'One
day…'; yours opens mid-complaint"* teaches; *"nice opening"* does not. Tell them
to keep it in the next draft, explicitly.
